Research Agenda and Current Projects

The Cosmotechnical Rhetoric of Algorithmic Mediation: Mathematical Spaces from Platonic Cosmology to Machine-Learning Algorithms

My dissertation examines mathematics as a mode of mediation, investigating how mathematical reasoning, formal abstraction, and algorithmic procedures shape the conditions under which meaning is produced and interpreted. Tracing conceptual relationships between Platonic cosmology and contemporary machine-learning systems, the project considers how mathematical structures organize representations, mediate knowledge, and establish the conditions of intelligibility. It approaches algorithmic systems not simply as technical instruments but as mathematical and rhetorical infrastructures through which meaning is organized, generated, and interpreted.

Methodologies

Drawing on rhetorical theory, media philosophy, the history and philosophy of mathematics, and Yuk Hui's concept of cosmotechnics, this research investigates the relationship between mathematical thought, representation, and computation. Its methodological approach combines philosophical close reading, mathematical historiography, conceptual analysis, and critical engagement with contemporary machine-learning research. Particular attention is given to the historical and cosmological conditions through which mathematics is conceptualized, the formal and spatial structures underlying computational representation, and the relationship between numerical operations and the production of meaning. Rather than treating mathematics as either a transparent representation of reality or a purely social construction, the project examines mathematics as a form of thought whose operations and representations participate in the constitution of intelligibility.

Research Directions

“From Plato's Khôra to Higher-Dimensional Vector Spaces: J-Spaces in Large Language Models as Khôral Images.” This essay reads Plato's account of khôra in the Timaeus alongside contemporary research in mechanistic interpretability, particularly the identification of J-space as a representational structure within large language models. It argues that the more fundamental conceptual relationship lies between khôra and the higher-dimensional vector spaces underlying computational representations. By examining mathematical space as a relational environment through which representations become intelligible, the essay reconsiders the role of intermediate mathematical structures in algorithmic mediation. Proposal accepted for a special issue of Angelaki: Journal of the Theoretical Humanities; manuscript in preparation.

“The Image of Mathematics in the Age of Machine Learning: A Cosmotechnical Inquiry.” This essay investigates the historically constituted image of mathematics and its relationship to contemporary machine-learning research. Taking the Platonic Representation Hypothesis as a point of departure, it revisits Plato's Meno to examine mathematical reasoning as a process involving intuition, error, formalization, and correction. Drawing on cosmotechnics, mathematical historiography, and the philosophy of mathematics, the essay distinguishes mathematics as a form of thought from conceptions of mathematics as a transparent mirror of reality, with implications for mathematics education and critical AI literacy. Manuscript in preparation.

Algorithmic Desire and the Psychoanalysis of Human–Computer Interfaces

My research examines how computational interfaces mediate human desire, subjectivity, and the interpretation of algorithmic systems. Focusing on generative artificial intelligence, recommendation systems, and digital platforms, this work investigates the relationship between the mathematical operations of computational systems and the linguistic, psychological, and cultural conditions through which users encounter them. Rather than treating interfaces as neutral surfaces, I approach them as sites where mathematical formalization, signification, and subjective experience intersect. This research asks how algorithmic systems participate in the organization of desire and how their apparent coherence, responsiveness, and personalization shape human relationships with computational technologies.

Methodologies

This research brings Lacanian psychoanalysis into conversation with rhetorical theory, media philosophy, human–computer interaction, and computational hermeneutics. It builds on Marc Heimann and Anne-Friederike Hübener's work on Lacanian signification and large language models, as well as Lorenzo Magnani's account of LLMs as systems of symbolic production without the constitutive lack associated with human subjectivity. Drawing on James E. Dobson's concept of vector hermeneutics, I examine how mathematical operations construct linguistic relationships and how those relationships become objects of human interpretation. Tracy McNulty's account of speculative fetishism provides a further framework for investigating the relationship between mathematical formalization, absence, and the apparent completeness of computational representations. These approaches inform philosophical close reading, rhetorical analysis of interfaces, and critical interpretation of machine-learning architectures and recommendation systems.

Research Directions

“Towards the Fetish Interface: Psychoanalytic Approaches to Vector Hermeneutics for Large Language Models.” Extending Dobson's vector hermeneutics through Lacanian psychoanalysis, this project examines how mathematical formalization mediates both the operations of language models and the interpretation of their outputs. In conversation with Heimann and Hübener's work on the relational structure of signification and Magnani's distinction between symbolic fluency and subjective desire, it turns to McNulty's theory of speculative fetishism to investigate the fetishistic function of mathematical representation. Rather than characterizing vector embeddings themselves as fetishistic, the project asks how mathematical formalization acquires the capacity to disclose linguistic relationships while obscuring the conditions of their production. It develops the concept of the fetish interface to examine the relationship between computational coherence, mathematical representation, and human interpretation.

“Algorithmic Radicalization and Desire.” This project draws on Lacan's reconsideration of Freud's pleasure and reality principles to examine the relationship between algorithmic recommendation, mathematical representation, and the formation of desire. Moving beyond explanations of radicalization centered exclusively on platform incentives or biased training data, it investigates how numerical representations of linguistic relationships structure possibilities for interpretation and engagement. By reading recommendation systems through their underlying mathematical environments, the project examines the relationship between algorithmic mediation, subjectivity, and the production of social and political meaning. Conference paper.

Risk Communication and the Administrative Rhetoric of Temporary Protected Status

Originating in my M.A. thesis, this ongoing research examines how technical and administrative documents construct risk, communicate humanitarian protection, and regulate migrant populations through institutional classifications. Focusing on the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and its administration of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for Salvadoran nationals, the project investigates the rhetorical and material consequences of federal immigration documentation. It traces the relationship between the language of humanitarian relief and the regulatory procedures through which displaced populations are classified, granted temporary protections, and excluded from pathways to permanent residence.

The initial study analyzes Federal Register notices establishing El Salvador's TPS designation following the 2001 earthquakes, extending that designation in 2016, announcing its termination in 2018, and rescinding the termination in 2023. It argues that the notices' sustained emphasis on the earthquakes functions as a bureaucratic justification that obscures the longer history of Salvadoran displacement, including U.S. involvement in the Salvadoran Civil War, the denial of humanitarian protection during the 1980s, and the consequences of U.S. deportation policies in the 1990s. By examining how administrative documents define eligibility, justify policy decisions, and delimit institutional responsibility, the project situates immigration regulation within technical communication's longstanding concerns with documentation, risk, accessibility, and social justice.

Methodologies

This research employs critical discourse analysis, rhetorical analysis of technical and regulatory documents, and historical analysis of U.S. immigration policy. Drawing on Teun A. van Dijk's approach to critical discourse analysis, it examines how institutional power and social inequality are reproduced and contested through administrative language. It also builds on technical communication scholarship concerning legal literacy, decolonial methodologies, and the social consequences of documentation, particularly the work of Godwin Y. Agboka, Angela M. Haas, Mark A. Hannah, and Natasha N. Jones and Miriam F. Williams. Research on migration and legal precarity, including the work of Cecilia Menjívar and Susan Bibler Coutin, informs its analysis of how bureaucratic classifications shape the lives of people subject to them.

Research Directions

“Conditions of Protection: Risk Communication and U.S.–Salvadoran Statecraft in Federal Register Notices of Temporary Protected Status.”

Building on several years of research into Salvadoran TPS, this article examines the consequences of DHS's failure to publish a determination when El Salvador's designation reached its scheduled expiration on September 9, 2026. Although USCIS subsequently indicated that beneficiaries would retain protection and employment authorization pending further notice, the absence of a corresponding Federal Register decision introduced uncertainty into the documentation used by TPS recipients, employers, and other institutions to establish legal and employment eligibility.

The article extends the earlier analysis of administrative justification to consider what happens when the expected regulatory document is not issued. It situates this development alongside the Supreme Court's 2026 decision in Mullin v. Doe, which substantially limited judicial review of nonconstitutional challenges to TPS determinations. Through the lenses of risk communication, institutional accountability, and social justice, the project investigates how administrative silence, inconsistent guidance, and constrained avenues of legal review redistribute the burden of uncertainty onto populations dependent on temporary humanitarian protection. Article in preparation for Technical Communication and Social Justice.
